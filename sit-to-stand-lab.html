Patient case: Henrietta

Before her stroke, Mrs. Henrietta T., a 54-year-old, lived independently with her husband and adult son. She enjoyed composing and performing songs, gardening, and socializing. She had an ischemic stroke affecting her left internal capsule, resulting in right hemiparesis. She spent 5 days in the hospital and 2 weeks in an inpatient rehabilitation unit, and she now receives outpatient therapy. She is gradually regaining the ability to stand, walk, and do personal activities of daily living (ADL) such as feeding, grooming, and dressing, but she has not regained her prestroke function.

Internal capsule: a V-shaped band of white matter deep in each cerebral hemisphere, between the thalamus and caudate nucleus on the inside and the lentiform nucleus (putamen and globus pallidus) on the outside. Ascending and descending fibers pass through it between the cerebral cortex and the brainstem and spinal cord, so a small stroke here can affect movement and sensation on the opposite side of the body.
Think about: Which factors support her recovery, and which constrain it? How much is spontaneous recovery, and how much is therapy? How much of what she learns in rehabilitation will she keep at home?

Motor learning: performance versus learning

At the end of her daily session, Henrietta sits and stands symmetrically, with weight evenly distributed on both legs. When she returns the next day, she again stands with all her weight on her noninvolved leg. Later, she begins arriving at therapy already standing more symmetrically.
